My partner is a professional chef. I'm a software engineer. We've discovered, over years of late-night conversations, that we're basically doing the same thing.

## Hypothesis-driven troubleshooting

When a sauce breaks, a good cook doesn't start over. They taste it, observe the texture, consider what they added last, and form a hypothesis. Too much acid? Not enough fat? Did the emulsion get too hot?

This is exactly how experienced programmers debug. You don't just read the stack trace — you taste the output, observe the behavior, consider what changed recently, and form a hypothesis.

## Mise en place is just setup

Chefs organize their ingredients before cooking. Programmers set up their dev environment before coding. Both disciplines have learned the same lesson: preparation reduces errors. When everything is in its place, you can focus on the creative work instead of scrambling for missing pieces.

## Recipes are documentation (with the same problems)

Recipes leave things out. "Season to taste." "Cook until done." "A pinch of salt." These are the culinary equivalent of "configuration left as an exercise for the reader."

Good recipes, like good documentation, are opinionated. They tell you exactly how much salt, exactly what temperature, exactly how long. They've been tested. They account for the ways beginners will misunderstand them.

## The taste-test feedback loop

The best cooks taste constantly. They adjust as they go. They don't follow the recipe blindly and hope for the best at the end.

The best programmers do the same — running code frequently, checking intermediate outputs, writing assertions that catch problems early. The tighter the feedback loop, the better the result.

## Where the analogy breaks down

Cooking has one advantage software doesn't: it's done when it's done. A meal gets served, eaten, and it's over. Software lives forever, accumulating complexity like a pot that never gets washed.

Maybe that's why programmers envy cooks. We want the satisfaction of a finished thing.
